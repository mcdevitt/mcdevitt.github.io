About Me

Welcome! I'm a biology education researcher who studies undergraduate research experiences and Course-based Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs). Much of my work focuses on understanding how students learn through authentic scientific inquiry and how we can make those opportunities available to more students. While my current work centers on introductory biology education, my path here began with a simple question: what is it about some research experiences that makes them so transformative for students?

My path into biology education research began at Penn State University, where I majored in Biology (Ecology & Evolution) while also pursuing a minor in Architectural History. Like many undergraduates, I had some exposure to research, but the experience that had the biggest impact on me was serving as an undergraduate teaching assistant for BISCI 003: Environmental Science. The course challenged students to think critically about environmental issues, sustainability, and their relationship with the natural world while emphasizing discussion, reflection, and peer-facilitated learning. Looking back, it felt like an early precursor to many of the active-learning and peer-support models that would later become common in STEM education.

After graduating from Penn State, I was preparing for a Peace Corps assignment focused on sustainable infrastructure projects in West Africa and spending time stateside brushing up on my language skills before deployment. During that gap, I accepted what I thought would be a temporary position at Juniata College's Raystown Field Station. Instead, regional instability prevented my assignment from moving forward, and that temporary job turned into a three-year appointment as Resident Director. At Raystown, I worked alongside undergraduates conducting wildlife management, environmental science, and field ecology research. While I enjoyed the science, I found myself becoming increasingly fascinated by the students themselves. Watching students gain confidence, overcome setbacks, and begin to see themselves as scientists led me to ask a question that still drives much of my work today: what is it about some undergraduate research experiences that makes them so transformative? Those questions ultimately inspired me to pursue graduate school and begin studying undergraduate research experiences as a biology education researcher.

To explore the questions that emerged from my time at Raystown, I pursued an M.S. in Biology at Illinois State University under the mentorship of Dr. Rebekka Darner. My graduate training combined conservation biology with biology education research focused on undergraduate research experiences, reflecting my growing interest in not only studying ecological systems, but also understanding how students learn science through authentic research. During this time, I became involved with the Harvard Forest Summer Research Program in Ecology (NSF REU), first as a Harvard Forest Fellow and later as a Harvard Forest Associate. Although I spent only two summers on-site at Harvard Forest, I remained involved for several years afterward as a biology education researcher. Working with students, mentors, and program leaders across multiple cohorts gave me a unique opportunity to study undergraduate research experiences from both practitioner and researcher perspectives. Those experiences deepened my interest in understanding how research experiences shape students' confidence, scientific identity, and career trajectories.

Those experiences led me to pursue a Ph.D. in Integrative and Systems Biology at the University of Colorado Denver under the mentorship of Dr. Laurel Hartley. My doctoral training provided a foundation in discipline-based biology education research, where I studied active learning, peer-assisted instruction, and Learning Assistant (LA)-supported STEM classrooms. Like many researchers, I found my work disrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic as the interactive, in-person classrooms I was studying suddenly disappeared. This challenge ultimately broadened my research interests and led me toward Course-based Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs), including projects in microbial ecology and urban wildlife ecology. During the latter part of my Ph.D. and my subsequent postdoctoral appointment in the Office of Inclusive Excellence in STEM, I assumed leadership of CU Denver's introductory biology laboratory program and its participation in the Urban Wildlife Information Network (UWIN) CURE. These experiences reinforced my belief that authentic research experiences should not be limited to a small number of students working in faculty laboratories, but can be thoughtfully integrated into introductory courses in ways that reach entire cohorts of students.

In 2024, I joined the University of Illinois Chicago in the Department of Biological Sciences. Today, I teach BIOS 110 and lead the development of a scalable 16S genomics CURE that provides authentic research experiences to more than 1,000 students each semester. My research and teaching now focus on understanding how students learn through authentic research and how those experiences can be integrated into introductory biology courses at scale.

Teaching Philosophy & Classroom Community Aligned with AAAS Vision and Change

"I Don't Teach You Anything—Instead, I Help You Learn"

In BIOS 110, we explore life from biological molecules and metabolic pathways to cellular systems and organismal physiology. Rather than treating introductory biology as an exercise in memorizing isolated facts, I approach my role as a learning coach—designing transparent, structured, and collaborative experiences where making mistakes is normalized as an essential part of scientific thinking.

Anyone Can Be a Scientist

Curiosity, effort, and openness to learning are what make a scientist—not prior lab experience or background.

Transparent Path to Success

Every lecture outline, reflection, Lab Skill Builder, and assessment is purposefully aligned so expectations are always clear.

Growth Over Grades

Success and joy in learning are not mutually exclusive—your development as a thinker, learner, and person comes first.

16-Week Course Progression

Built around free OpenStax Biology 2e materials, interactive iClicker Cloud sessions, and weekly metacognitive reflections:

  • Weeks 1–5 (Foundations): Scientific skills, biological chemistry, cellular structure, microscopy, and enzyme kinetics.
  • Weeks 6–10 (Genomics CURE): Cell division, DNA structure, protein synthesis, DNA extraction, PCR, gel electrophoresis, and 16S rRNA sequencing of commercial probiotics.
  • Weeks 11–15 (Bioenergetics & Systems): Membrane transport, photosynthesis, cellular respiration, and comparative animal physiology.

Instructional Team & Student Flexibility

Large courses thrive when students have multiple entry points for mentorship, peer collaboration, and equitable course policies:

  • Peer Learning Assistants (PLAs): Experienced undergraduates who recently excelled in BIOS 110 host drop-in sessions in UIC’s Math and Science Learning Center (MSLC).
  • Lab Teaching Assistants (14–18 TAs/semester): Post-bac and graduate TAs trained in dialogic facilitation guide 30+ weekly lab sections.
  • Lab Skill Builders & Drop Policies: Scaffolded online skill builders let students stay on track after an excused absence without losing CURE momentum.

Course-Based Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs)

Traditional apprentice-style undergraduate research positions are often limited by lab capacity. My work focuses on designing, scaling, and evaluating authentic, process-oriented CUREs embedded directly into high-enrollment introductory biology courses—giving every student hands-on experience with experimental controls, technical replication, bioinformatics/R pipelines, and scientific troubleshooting.

UIC • August 2025 – Present ~1,100 Students / Semester • 33 Lab Sections

BIOS 110 Probiotics & 16S Genomics CURE

A scalable molecular and genomics workflow introducing introductory biology students to iterative experimentation and data analysis. Across 33 lab sections each semester, students practice core bench techniques, extract genomic DNA from commercial probiotic supplements, perform PCR and gel electrophoresis, and prepare samples for 16S rRNA sequencing. Students analyze microbial community sequence data against spike-in controls and evaluate variation across peers as technical replicates—emphasizing process-oriented thinking, quality control (QA/QC), troubleshooting, and interpretation of experimental variability.

Classroom Simulations

Complex biological systems—from cellular dynamics to evolutionary and ecological processes—are governed by stochasticity, feedback loops, and emergent interactions that are difficult to grasp from static textbook diagrams alone. My work integrates formal ecological inference, NetLogo agent-based modeling, and lightweight browser-based HTML/JavaScript applications hosted on GitHub Pages so introductory biology students can actively test competing hypotheses and manipulate experimental parameters.

Pedagogical & Modeling Philosophy Mechanistic Modeling & Ecological Inference

Multiple Working Hypotheses & Simulation-Based Inference

Grounded in our peer-reviewed framework (Yanco et al., 2020), simulation-based instruction encourages students and researchers to move beyond single-hypothesis testing. By formulating multiple competing mechanistic models prior to interpreting data (“hypothesis vetting”) and comparing simulated system outcomes against empirical observations, students develop stronger quantitative reasoning, experimental troubleshooting, and inference skills.

Large-Enrollment STEM & the Learning Assistant (LA) Model

Ph.D. Dissertation (2022) • University of Colorado Denver (Advisor: Dr. Laurel Hartley) Integrative & Systems Biology

Advancing Understanding of Student-Centered Instruction in Undergraduate STEM by Promoting the Connection of Theory with Analytical Techniques

Active learning is consistently linked to higher student success in undergraduate STEM, yet understanding why active learning works, in what contexts, and for whom requires connecting explicit learning theory with rigorous classroom observation methodologies. My dissertation—followed by postdoctoral work in CU Denver’s Office of Inclusive Excellence in STEM (2022–2024)—examined Learning Assistant (LA) supported STEM courses across three core themes that shape how I prepare Peer Learning Assistants (PLAs) and graduate Teaching Assistants (TAs) today.

Chapter I • Modified COPUS 51 Courses

Learning Assistant Supported STEM Courses Exhibit More Student-Centered Course Structure Compared to a National Sample

Analyzed 287 class sessions across 51 introductory biology, chemistry, and physics courses at three universities using a modified 37-code Classroom Observation Protocol for Undergraduate STEM (COPUS) separating LA, instructor, and student behaviors.

LA-supported courses exhibited 69.0% student-centered instruction compared to 18.6% in the North American benchmark sample. Instructors led lecture and whole-class discussion, whereas LAs guided small groups and one-on-one discussions nearly as frequently as instructors during active work.

Instructional Implication

Near-peer LAs/PLAs multiply guided feedback during active problem-solving and lab work without replacing faculty framing.

Chapter II • Sociocultural Lens Mediated Action

Mediated Action: A Theory to Support Understanding of Active Learning

Applies Wertsch’s sociocultural theory of mediated action and 75-minute stimulated recall faculty interviews to investigate who is learning, what is learned, why, and how classroom artifacts and dialogue mediate growth.

Comparing two courses with nearly identical COPUS profiles and concept-inventory gains revealed distinct faculty intentions—one emphasizing iterative conceptual mastery and another prioritizing science identity, belonging, and dismantling “weed-out” norms.

Instructional Implication

Worksheets and activities depend on facilitation; training TAs and PLAs to probe student reasoning supports both conceptual and affective outcomes.

Chapter III • Quantitative Methods Monte Carlo (R)

Classroom Observations: How Often Do We Need to Observe?

Built Monte Carlo simulation frameworks in R to evaluate how observation design decisions—observation window duration (1 second to 5 minutes), misclassification error (false positives vs. false negatives), baseline behavior frequency, and sampling strategy—govern parameter precision.

Demonstrated how 2-minute intervals inflate estimates of brief behaviors, why behaviors near 50% frequency carry the widest confidence intervals, and when cluster or systematic sampling must account for course structure.

Instructional Implication

Aligning observation sampling intensity with explicit formative feedback or evaluation goals prevents over-interpreting limited classroom snapshots.
